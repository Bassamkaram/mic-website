Notes from the campus
on building in Lebanon.

Honest writing for founders who've stopped expecting easy. No motivational fluff — just what we and the people around us have learned the hard way.

Ecosystem · 5 min read · May 2026

Lebanon's diaspora is the secret weapon nobody quite knows how to use.

There are roughly fifteen million Lebanese living outside Lebanon. There are fewer than five million inside it.

The diaspora is, mathematically, the country. We should start building like it.

Walk into any tech hub from São Paulo to Singapore, Lagos to Los Angeles, and you'll find a Lebanese name on a senior nameplate. Engineers at Google, partners at Sequoia, founders of Series C startups, professors at INSEAD. They built the careers we read about. Then they kept them.

What's strange is how little their networks, capital, and operating knowledge flow back. Not because the diaspora doesn't care — most care deeply — but because nobody on the ground has built a credible interface. The mechanism for a Lebanese-American product leader to spend two hours a month helping a Beirut founder simply doesn't exist. So they help when asked, awkwardly, in ad-hoc ways, and the rest gets lost.

The institutions that should bridge this gap have mostly failed at it. Government programs are noisy and untrusted. University alumni networks are warm but professionally underbuilt. Investment vehicles aimed at the diaspora have come and gone, usually because they couldn't show enough deal flow to justify the wire.

The opportunity is to build a permanent, productive interface. Not another conference or LinkedIn group — a place where serious people can give serious time, on terms that respect both their schedule and the founder's stage. A campus, a roster of vetted companies, structured engagement formats: office hours, advisory roles, syndicate checks.

The diaspora isn't a marketing asset. It's an operating asset. We should treat it like one.

— The MIC editorial team

Operating · 4 min read · April 2026

Building in Lebanon: five things the last five years taught us.

If you've shipped product in Lebanon between 2019 and 2024, you've earned a graduate degree in operational improvisation.

Here's the syllabus, in five lessons.

One: Cash is not a strategy, but it is oxygen. Founders who survived kept enough fresh USD on hand to operate for at least six months without revenue. Those who didn't, learned the hard way that banking restrictions are not theoretical.

Two: Hire for resilience, not just resume. The most valuable people on a Lebanese team between 2019 and 2024 weren't the highest pedigree — they were the ones who showed up when the road was closed, the power was out, the salary was late, and the country was on television for the wrong reasons.

Three: Build for the region, sell to the region, take payment from anywhere. Lebanese founders who tried to operate as a Lebanon-only business saw their addressable market shrink by half. Those who positioned regionally from day one had options.

Four: Process over heroics. Heroics scale to about six people, then they break. The teams that endured built dull, boring, reliable internal systems — written playbooks, regular check-ins, clear ownership — precisely because the external environment was anything but.

Five: Document everything. Founders who left for the diaspora kept their best work alive only when there was written context behind it. Tribal knowledge dies the day someone takes a one-way flight, and a lot of one-way flights happened.

The lessons aren't pretty. But the founders who internalized them are now operating with a quiet edge most peer ecosystems lack.

— The MIC editorial team

Location · 4 min read · March 2026

Beyond Beirut: why the Metn is becoming Lebanon's innovation corridor.

Beirut got the headlines for a generation. The next chapter of Lebanese entrepreneurship is being written ten kilometers east, in the towns and suburbs of Mount Lebanon.

If you spent the 2010s in Lebanese tech, every meeting happened in Hamra, Mar Mikhael, or Gemmayze. Beirut was the gravity. Cool was synonymous with downtown. Office leases priced accordingly.

That gravity has shifted. The reasons are partly tragic — the August 4 explosion, the financial collapse, the rolling exodus — but the result is structural. Where founders live, work, and build has moved, and the Metn has absorbed the most concentrated share.

Three things are pulling people east. First, it's where families ended up: schools, longer leases, and the kind of housing density that supports a working professional class. Second, infrastructure: reliable electricity provision through neighborhood échitirâk, water that arrives when it's scheduled, internet that mostly stays up. Third, cost: office space in Jdeideh, Antelias, or Dora runs at a fraction of what equivalent square footage costs in Beirut proper.

What's missing — until now — is dedicated infrastructure for builders. Coffee shops have done heroic work as substitute offices for years, but they're not a substitute for a campus designed around the work. The Metn has the people, the families, the residential density, and the operating reliability. What it needs is somewhere for those people to gather, collide, and ship.

That's the bet behind MIC: that the next Lebanese tech generation will be Metn-based, not Beirut-based, and that an intentionally-designed campus in Jdeideh will be one of the places that generation chooses to call work.

Playbook · 6 min read · February 2026

From idea to MVP in Lebanon: a practical playbook.

Most idea-stage founders don't fail because the idea is bad. They fail because they spent six months building before they spent six weeks asking.

Here's an honest sequence we've watched work for first-time Lebanese founders.

Weeks 1–3: Talk to twenty people. Not friends. People who actually have the problem you think you're solving. Buy them coffee. Ask them how they handle it today. If you can't find twenty in three weeks, your problem is either too niche or doesn't really exist — better to know now.

Weeks 4–6: Build the cheapest possible thing. A Google Form, a WhatsApp group, a manual service you fulfill yourself. Anything that lets you charge or commit a customer. The goal is to feel the friction of real exchange before you write a line of production code.

Weeks 7–10: Get five paying users. Not five sign-ups. Five people who paid you actual money — even five dollars — for the cheap thing. Lebanese founders often skip this step because "the market is too small to pay." It's not. They're usually selling to the wrong segment.

Weeks 11–14: Build the real MVP. Now you build software. You know the problem, you've felt the workflow, you've heard your users complain in their own words. Building is easier when you're translating something you already understand — not guessing.

Weeks 15–16: Launch publicly. Reflect honestly. Most Lebanese founders we've seen succeed at this stage have one trait in common: they took written notes through the entire fourteen weeks. They have a paper trail of what they thought, what they learned, what changed.

That paper trail becomes the pitch deck. Don't skip it.

— The MIC editorial team

Capital · 5 min read · January 2026

Raising capital as a Lebanese founder: the rules are different.

If your fundraising playbook came from Y Combinator's library, congratulations — you've learned the wrong game.

Here's the one we actually play.

The standard Silicon Valley narrative goes like this: build something users love, show steep early growth, send a deck to twenty VCs, run a hot process, close a round in six weeks. None of that translates cleanly to a Lebanese founder raising in 2026.

The first reality: regional and diaspora investors care more about operating quality than growth curve. Lebanese founders raising from MENA funds or diaspora angels are evaluated on whether they can survive turbulent operating environments — not whether they can hockey-stick under perfect conditions. Show resilience, not vanity.

The second reality: warm introductions matter more than they should. The Beirut-to-Riyadh-to-Dubai investor network runs on personal trust, and a cold deck without a warm intro will sit unread. Spend the first month of your raise getting introductions lined up, not polishing your slides.

The third reality: structure your raise in fresh USD and through an offshore entity if possible. Lebanese investors and the diaspora can wire to Cyprus, Delaware, or Dubai. They generally cannot wire into Lebanese banks. Founders who waited to set this up after a term sheet have lost deals to weeks of legal scrambling.

The fourth reality: take smart money, not just any money. A check from a regional family office or a diaspora operator who can open doors is worth twice the same amount from a passive local investor. In a small ecosystem, the wrong cap table becomes a brand problem you carry for years.

Fundraising as a Lebanese founder is harder. It's also — if done right — a forcing function for the operating discipline that becomes your unfair advantage later.

— The MIC editorial team
